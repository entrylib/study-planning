CLAIMSET Evaluating forecasts and classifiers: which measures, scoring rules and tests to use
summary How should a study measure and compare predictions? This review states what the methodological literature establishes and the rules it draws from it. A measure has to match what a prediction targets: squared error (RMSE) for forecasts of the mean, absolute error (MAE) for the median, the pinball loss for a quantile, the interval score with coverage for intervals, CRPS or the log score for distributions, and log loss or the Brier score for class probabilities. MAPE rewards forecasts that are too low, sMAPE prefers forecasts that are too high, and AUC and accuracy do not judge probabilities. Errors of series on different scales should be scaled per series before they are averaged, never summed as they are. The mean, median and quartiles of a scaled measure summarise accuracy over many series; rank-based tests (Friedman with Nemenyi, MCB) and the Diebold-Mariano test tell whether similar methods differ statistically.

DEFINITIONS
D1 [concept] consistent scoring function: A scoring function (an error measure for point forecasts) is consistent for a functional of the predictive distribution, such as the mean, the median or a quantile, when its expected value is lowest when the point forecast equals that functional. [source B2]
D2 [concept] proper scoring rule: A scoring rule for probabilistic forecasts is proper when its expected value is best when the forecast distribution equals the distribution of the outcome, and strictly proper when it is best only then. [source B3]
D3 [concept] point forecast of the mean: A single-valued forecast meant to equal the mean (expected value) of the outcome's distribution given the information available. [source B2]
D4 [concept] point forecast of the median: A single-valued forecast meant to equal the median of the outcome's distribution given the information available. [source B2]
D5 [variable] RMSE: Root mean squared error: the square root of the mean of the squared differences between outcomes and point forecasts. [source B6]
D6 [variable] RMSSE: Root mean squared scaled error: the RMSE of a series' forecasts divided by the in-sample RMSE of the one-step naive forecast of that series, so that series on different scales can be averaged. [source B9]
D7 [variable] MAE: Mean absolute error: the mean of the absolute differences between outcomes and point forecasts. [source B6]
D8 [variable] MASE: Mean absolute scaled error: the MAE of a series' forecasts divided by the in-sample MAE of the one-step naive forecast of that series. [source B6]
D9 [variable] MAPE: Mean absolute percentage error: the mean of the absolute differences between outcomes and point forecasts, each divided by the outcome. [source B6]
D10 [variable] sMAPE: Symmetric mean absolute percentage error: the mean of the absolute differences between outcomes and point forecasts, each divided by the mean of the absolute outcome and the absolute forecast. [source B11]
D11 [concept] median of order -1: The beta-median with beta = -1: the point forecast that minimises the expected absolute percentage error; for positive outcomes it lies below the median. [source B2]
D12 [variable] pinball loss: The quantile (asymmetric piecewise linear) loss at level tau: tau times the error when the outcome is above the forecast, and (1 - tau) times the absolute error when it is below. [source B2]
D13 [variable] interval score: The score of a central (1 - alpha) prediction interval: its width plus 2/alpha times the distance by which the outcome falls outside it. [source B3]
D14 [variable] coverage: The share of outcomes that fall inside the prediction intervals, compared with their nominal level. [source B3]
D15 [variable] CRPS: Continuous ranked probability score: the integral over thresholds of the squared difference between the forecast's cumulative distribution function and the step function at the outcome. [source B3]
D16 [variable] log score: The logarithmic score: the negative logarithm of the forecast density (or probability) at the outcome. [source B3]
D17 [variable] Brier score: The mean squared difference between forecast probabilities of a class and the outcome coded 1 if the class occurred and 0 otherwise. [source B13]
D18 [concept] probability integral transform: The value of the forecast's cumulative distribution function at the outcome; for a calibrated forecast its values are uniformly distributed. [source B4]
D19 [concept] calibration: Statistical consistency between forecast distributions and outcomes: events forecast with a given probability occur with that frequency. [source B4]
D20 [concept] sharpness: The concentration of the forecast distributions, a property of the forecasts alone; among calibrated forecasts, sharper ones are better. [source B4]
D21 [variable] accuracy: The share of cases whose predicted class, after thresholding the predicted probabilities, equals the observed class.
D22 [variable] AUC: The area under the ROC curve: the probability that a randomly chosen case of the positive class receives a higher score than a randomly chosen case of the negative class. [source B14]
D23 [concept] precision-recall curve: The curve of precision (the share of predicted positives that are positive) against recall (the share of positives predicted as positive) over the classification thresholds. [source B7]
D24 [data] intermittent series: A time series with many zero values, such as the sales of a slow-moving item. [source B12]
D25 [method] Friedman test: A non-parametric test of whether several methods differ, applied to their ranks on each of several data sets. [source B5]
D26 [method] Nemenyi test: A post-hoc test after the Friedman test that compares every pair of methods by their mean ranks against a critical difference. [source B5]
D27 [method] MCB: Multiple comparisons with the best: a test, on mean ranks over series, of which methods differ significantly from the best-ranked one. [source B15]
D28 [method] Diebold-Mariano test: A test of equal expected loss of two competing forecasts of the same series, based on the mean loss differential and its long-run variance. [source B16]

WARRANTS
W1 [deductive; premises C30] A forecast is built to target one functional, and only a scoring function consistent for that functional is lowest in expectation for it; a scoring function consistent for another functional ranks forecasts by how close they come to that other functional.
W2 [deductive; premises C29, C30] Squared error is consistent for the mean, so its expected value is lowest for the forecast that equals the mean; the RMSE, the square root of the mean squared error, ranks forecasts in the same order.
W3 [deductive; premises C47, C29, C46] Dividing a series' squared errors by a fixed positive number, the in-sample mean squared error of its one-step naive forecast, does not change which forecast minimises their expected value, so the RMSSE keeps the target of the RMSE, the mean, and the scaled values of different series can be averaged, as in the M5 accuracy competition.
W4 [deductive; premises C31, C29] Absolute error, which MAE averages and MASE scales, is consistent for the median, not the mean; on skewed outcomes the two differ, and these measures then rank a median forecast above a forecast that is correct for the mean.
W5 [deductive; premises C32] MAPE is consistent for the median of order -1, which supports severe underforecasts compared with the mean or the median; it therefore rewards forecasts that are too low for either target.
W6 [deductive; premises C33] sMAPE penalises forecasts below the outcome more heavily than forecasts above it, so it rewards forecasts that are too high; and it divides by the mean of the absolute outcome and the absolute forecast, which is zero when both are zero, so it is then undefined.
W7 [deductive; premises C31, C46] Absolute error is consistent for the median, so its expected value is lowest for the median forecast; MASE divides each series' absolute errors by a fixed in-sample quantity, which keeps that target and lets series on different scales be averaged.
W8 [deductive; premises C41, C31] On intermittent series the median of the outcome can be zero, and absolute error, consistent for the median, then gives its best score to a forecast of zero in every period; a measure of the mean or of the whole distribution shows what such a forecast misses.
W9 [deductive; premises C34] The pinball loss at a level is consistent for the quantile at that level, so its expected value is lowest for the correct quantile; averaging over the levels gives each quantile the same weight.
W10 [deductive; premises C35] The interval score is proper for central intervals and addresses width as well as coverage; coverage against the nominal level shows how far the intervals' calibration departs from what they claim.
W11 [deductive; premises C35] Coverage counts only whether each outcome falls inside its interval; widening an interval never lowers its coverage, so coverage alone cannot penalise needless width, which the interval score does.
W12 [deductive; premises C36, C37] The CRPS and the log score are proper: their expected values are best for the true distribution, so they never reward a forecaster for reporting a distribution other than the one believed.
W13 [deductive; premises C39] Predictive distributions are assessed by maximising their sharpness subject to calibration; checking calibration, with the probability integral transform or coverage at several levels, and reporting sharpness show how a forecast fails, which a single score does not.
W14 [deductive; premises C40, C36, C37] The probability integral transform of a discrete distribution takes few values and is not uniform even when the forecast is calibrated, so the calibration of count forecasts is checked with the randomised transform; proper scoring rules apply to count distributions as to any other.
W15 [deductive; premises C38, C37] The Brier score is proper and the log score strictly proper, so both are best in expectation for the true probabilities of the classes.
W16 [deductive; premises C38, C37] Accuracy uses a forecast probability only through which side of a threshold it falls, and counts every misclassification as the same loss; two forecasts with different probabilities but the same thresholded labels score the same, and unequal costs of errors are not reflected. The Brier and log scores, which are proper, reward the probabilities themselves.
W17 [deductive; premises C42] The AUC averages the misclassification loss over a cost distribution that depends on the classifier, so different classifiers are compared with different weightings of the costs of errors; and it depends only on how the scores rank cases, not on whether the probabilities are right.
W18 [deductive; premises C43] On imbalanced data the ROC curve can be deceptive about how reliable a classifier is, and the precision-recall curve is the more informative of the two; reporting it shows what the ROC curve can hide.
W19 [deductive; premises C30] Each scoring function is lowest in expectation for the functional it is consistent for, so one measure favours the methods that target that functional; comparing methods that target different functionals with one measure compares their targets, not their skill.
W20 [deductive; premises C46, C50] A scaled measure can be averaged over series, but averages over many data sets are susceptible to outliers; the median and the quartiles show how accuracy is spread over the series, which the mean alone hides.
W21 [deductive; premises C51] The Friedman test, followed by the Nemenyi test, compares methods by their ranks on each data set and assumes only limited commensurability of the measure across data sets; it tells whether differences between methods whose aggregate accuracy is close are larger than chance.
W22 [deductive; premises C52] Multiple comparisons with the best on ranks test which methods differ from the best one, and were used for this on the methods of the M3 competition.
W23 [deductive; premises C53] The Diebold-Mariano test allows forecast errors that are serially correlated and not normally distributed, as the errors of multi-step forecasts of one series usually are; tests that assume independent, normal errors do not hold for them.
W24 [deductive; premises C30] A measure fits a study when it is consistent for what the compared methods predict; that a competition used a measure says nothing about whether it is consistent for the forecasts of this study.
W25 [deductive; premises C44, C45] Scale-dependent measures cannot be compared across series on different scales, and summed or averaged without scaling they are dominated by the series with the largest values; the result then says little about accuracy on the other series.
W26 [deductive; premises C44, C46, C47] Scaled errors are independent of the scale of the data and can be averaged over series; dividing by a quantity of the series that does not depend on the compared forecasts keeps the target that the unscaled measure is consistent for.
W27 [deductive; premises C48, C34, C44] Pinball losses of series on different scales cannot be compared unscaled; dividing each series' pinball loss by a quantity of that series that does not depend on the forecasts keeps the quantile it is consistent for, as the scaled pinball loss of the M5 uncertainty competition does.
W28 [deductive; premises C49, C35, C44] Interval scores of series on different scales cannot be compared unscaled; dividing each series' interval score by a positive quantity of that series that does not depend on the forecasts keeps it proper, as the mean scaled interval score of the M4 competition does.

CLAIMSET Good practice for evaluating predictions: what the literature supports
summary How should a study measure and compare predictions? This paper turns the methodological literature into rules. A measure has to match what a prediction targets: squared error (RMSE) for forecasts of the mean, absolute error (MAE) for the median, the pinball loss for a quantile, the interval score with coverage for intervals, and a proper scoring rule for distributions and class probabilities. MAPE rewards forecasts that are too low, sMAPE forecasts that are too high, and AUC and accuracy do not judge probabilities. Errors of series on different scales should be scaled per series before they are averaged (RMSSE, MASE, scaled pinball loss, scaled interval score), never summed as they are. The mean, median and quartiles of a scaled measure summarise accuracy over many series, and rank tests tell whether methods that perform similarly differ. Each rule cites the paper it rests on; several were checked only by abstracts.

DEFINITIONS
D1 [concept] consistent scoring function: A scoring function (an error measure for point forecasts) is consistent for a functional of the predictive distribution, such as the mean, the median or a quantile, when its expected value is lowest when the point forecast equals that functional. [source B1]
D2 [concept] proper scoring rule: A scoring rule for probabilistic forecasts is proper when its expected value is best when the forecast distribution equals the distribution of the outcome, and strictly proper when it is best only then. [source B2]
D3 [concept] point forecast of the mean: A single-valued forecast meant to equal the mean (expected value) of the outcome's distribution given the information available. [source B1]
D4 [concept] point forecast of the median: A single-valued forecast meant to equal the median of the outcome's distribution given the information available. [source B1]
D5 [variable] RMSE: Root mean squared error: the square root of the mean of the squared differences between outcomes and point forecasts. [source B7]
D6 [variable] RMSSE: Root mean squared scaled error: the RMSE of a series' forecasts divided by the in-sample RMSE of the one-step naive forecast of that series, so that series on different scales can be averaged. [source B10]
D7 [variable] MAE: Mean absolute error: the mean of the absolute differences between outcomes and point forecasts. [source B7]
D8 [variable] MASE: Mean absolute scaled error: the MAE of a series' forecasts divided by the in-sample MAE of the one-step naive forecast of that series. [source B7]
D9 [variable] MAPE: Mean absolute percentage error: the mean of the absolute differences between outcomes and point forecasts, each divided by the outcome. [source B7]
D10 [variable] sMAPE: Symmetric mean absolute percentage error: the mean of the absolute differences between outcomes and point forecasts, each divided by the mean of the absolute outcome and the absolute forecast. [source B12]
D11 [concept] median of order -1: The beta-median with beta = -1: the point forecast that minimises the expected absolute percentage error; for positive outcomes it lies below the median. [source B1]
D12 [variable] pinball loss: The quantile (asymmetric piecewise linear) loss at level tau: tau times the error when the outcome is above the forecast, and (1 - tau) times the absolute error when it is below. [source B1]
D13 [variable] interval score: The score of a central (1 - alpha) prediction interval: its width plus 2/alpha times the distance by which the outcome falls outside it. [source B2]
D14 [variable] coverage: The share of outcomes that fall inside the prediction intervals, compared with their nominal level. [source B2]
D15 [variable] CRPS: Continuous ranked probability score: the integral over thresholds of the squared difference between the forecast's cumulative distribution function and the step function at the outcome. [source B2]
D16 [variable] log score: The logarithmic score: the negative logarithm of the forecast density (or probability) at the outcome. [source B2]
D17 [variable] Brier score: The mean squared difference between forecast probabilities of a class and the outcome coded 1 if the class occurred and 0 otherwise. [source B14]
D18 [concept] probability integral transform: The value of the forecast's cumulative distribution function at the outcome; for a calibrated forecast its values are uniformly distributed. [source B3]
D19 [concept] calibration: Statistical consistency between forecast distributions and outcomes: events forecast with a given probability occur with that frequency. [source B3]
D20 [concept] sharpness: The concentration of the forecast distributions, a property of the forecasts alone; among calibrated forecasts, sharper ones are better. [source B3]
D21 [variable] accuracy: The share of cases whose predicted class, after thresholding the predicted probabilities, equals the observed class.
D22 [variable] AUC: The area under the ROC curve: the probability that a randomly chosen case of the positive class receives a higher score than a randomly chosen case of the negative class. [source B15]
D23 [concept] precision-recall curve: The curve of precision (the share of predicted positives that are positive) against recall (the share of positives predicted as positive) over the classification thresholds. [source B8]
D24 [data] intermittent series: A time series with many zero values, such as the sales of a slow-moving item. [source B13]
D25 [method] Friedman test: A non-parametric test of whether several methods differ, applied to their ranks on each of several data sets. [source B4]
D26 [method] Nemenyi test: A post-hoc test after the Friedman test that compares every pair of methods by their mean ranks against a critical difference. [source B4]
D27 [method] MCB: Multiple comparisons with the best: a test, on mean ranks over series, of which methods differ significantly from the best-ranked one. [source B16]
D28 [method] Diebold-Mariano test: A test of equal expected loss of two competing forecasts of the same series, based on the mean loss differential and its long-run variance. [source B17]

CLAIMS
C1 [methodological; principal; extension; support: cited B1, at Abstract; Section 2.2 (Consistency) (arXiv:0912.0902 numbering), cited B9; limitations L2, L3, L5; strength high (author); planning exploratory; outcome supported]
  To evaluate point forecasts, use a [[D1:consistent scoring function]] for the functional the forecasts target (the mean, the median or a quantile): a scoring function consistent for a different functional rewards a different forecast from the one the method was built to make.
  scope: data_type: point forecasts
  extends B1: Gneiting (2011) shows that a scoring function must be consistent for the functional a point forecast targets for the evaluation to reward that forecast.
C2 [methodological; principal; extension; support: cited B1, at Section 5 (Discussion); Theorem 3.1 (arXiv:0912.0902 numbering); uses C1; limitations L3, L5; strength high (author); planning exploratory; outcome supported]
  Point forecasts of the mean ([[D3:point forecast of the mean]]) should be evaluated with squared error ([[D5:RMSE]]), the scoring function consistent for the mean.
  scope: data_type: point forecasts of the mean
  extends B1: Gneiting (2011) shows that squared error is consistent for the mean.
C3 [methodological; principal; extension; support: cited B19, at Point forecasts (RMSSE), cited B10, argument W1; uses C2; limitations L2, L5; strength high (author); planning exploratory; outcome supported]
  When [[D3:point forecasts of the mean]] are compared over series on different scales, the [[D5:RMSE]] of each series should be scaled by the in-sample RMSE of the one-step naive forecast ([[D6:RMSSE]]), as the M5 accuracy competition did; scaling each series by a fixed number keeps the comparison consistent for the mean.
  scope: data_type: point forecasts of the mean, over series on different scales
  extends B19: The M5 competition scored point forecasts by the RMSSE, chosen because squared errors are optimised for the mean and the scaling makes series comparable.
C4 [methodological; principal; extension; support: cited B1, at Section 5 (Discussion) (arXiv:0912.0902 numbering); uses C1; limitations L3, L5; strength high (author); planning exploratory; outcome supported]
  [[D7:MAE]] and [[D8:MASE]] should not be the main measure for [[D3:point forecasts of the mean]]: absolute error is consistent for the median and rewards median forecasts.
  scope: data_type: point forecasts of the mean
  extends B1: Gneiting (2011) shows that absolute error is consistent for the median.
C5 [methodological; principal; extension; support: cited B1, at Section 5 (Discussion); beta-median in Section 1.3, MAPE as its beta = -1 case in Section 2.3 (arXiv:0912.0902 numbering); uses C1; limitations L3, L5; strength high (author); planning exploratory; outcome supported]
  [[D9:MAPE]] should not be the measure for point forecasts of the mean or the median: it is consistent for the [[D11:median of order -1]], which lies below the median for positive outcomes, so it rewards forecasts that are too low.
  scope: data_type: point forecasts of positive outcomes
  extends B1: Gneiting (2011) shows that the absolute percentage error is consistent for the median of order -1, which supports underforecasts.
C6 [methodological; principal; extension; support: cited B12, cited B7, at Section 2.2 (Measures based on percentage errors), argument W2; limitations L2, L3, L5; strength high (author); planning exploratory; outcome supported]
  [[D10:sMAPE]] should not be the measure for point forecasts: for the same outcome it penalises forecasts below the outcome more heavily than forecasts above it, so it rewards forecasts that are too high, and it is undefined when the outcome and the forecast are both zero.
  scope: data_type: point forecasts
  extends B12: Goodwin and Lawton (1999) show that the symmetric MAPE is not symmetric in its treatment of positive and negative errors of the same size.
C7 [methodological; principal; extension; support: cited B1, at Section 5 (Discussion) (arXiv:0912.0902 numbering), cited B7, at Abstract; Section 3 (Scaled errors); uses C1; limitations L3, L5; strength high (author); planning exploratory; outcome supported]
  Point forecasts of the median ([[D4:point forecast of the median]]) should be evaluated with absolute error ([[D7:MAE]]), the scoring function consistent for the median, and, across series on different scales, with [[D8:MASE]].
  scope: data_type: point forecasts of the median
  extends B7: Hyndman and Koehler (2006) propose the MASE as the standard measure across series.
C8 [methodological; principal; extension; support: cited B19, at Point forecasts (the choice of the RMSSE), cited B13; uses C4; limitations L2, L5; strength high (author); planning exploratory; outcome supported]
  On [[D24:intermittent series]], absolute-error measures should not be used alone: the median of the outcome can be zero, and absolute error then rewards a forecast of zero for every period.
  scope: data_type: point forecasts of intermittent series
  extends B19: The M5 guide states that on intermittent series absolute errors favour forecasts close to zero.
C9 [methodological; principal; extension; support: cited B1, at Abstract; Theorem 3.3 (arXiv:0912.0902 numbering); uses C1; limitations L3, L5; strength high (author); planning exploratory; outcome supported]
  Quantile forecasts should be evaluated with the [[D12:pinball loss]] at the quantile's level, the scoring function consistent for that quantile, averaged over the levels when there are several.
  scope: data_type: quantile forecasts
  extends B1: Gneiting (2011) shows that a scoring function is consistent for a quantile if and only if it is generalized piecewise linear.
C10 [methodological; principal; extension; support: cited B2, at Section 6.2 (Interval score); limitations L3, L5; strength high (author); planning exploratory; outcome supported]
  Prediction intervals should be evaluated with the [[D13:interval score]], a proper scoring rule for central intervals, reported together with their [[D14:coverage]] against the nominal level.
  scope: data_type: central prediction intervals
  extends B2: Gneiting and Raftery (2007) show that the interval score is proper for central prediction intervals.
C11 [methodological; principal; extension; support: cited B2, at Abstract; Section 6.2 (Interval score), argument W3; limitations L3, L5; strength high (author); planning exploratory; outcome supported]
  [[D14:coverage]] alone should not be the measure of prediction intervals: it does not measure their width, so sharp intervals and needlessly wide ones can reach the same coverage.
  scope: data_type: prediction intervals
  extends B2: Gneiting and Raftery (2007) propose the interval score because it addresses width as well as coverage.
C12 [methodological; principal; extension; support: cited B2, at Section 4.1 (log score); Section 4.2 (CRPS); limitations L3, L5; strength high (author); planning exploratory; outcome supported]
  Predictive distributions should be evaluated with a [[D2:proper scoring rule]], such as the [[D15:CRPS]] or the [[D16:log score]].
  scope: data_type: predictive distributions
  extends B2: Gneiting and Raftery (2007) show that the CRPS and the logarithmic score are proper.
C13 [methodological; principal; extension; support: cited B3, at Abstract; Section 3.1 (Assessing probabilistic calibration); Section 3.3 (Assessing sharpness); uses C12; limitations L3, L5; strength high (author); planning exploratory; outcome supported]
  The evaluation of predictive distributions should check their [[D19:calibration]], with the [[D18:probability integral transform]] or coverage at several levels, and report their [[D20:sharpness]]: the goal is the sharpest forecast that is calibrated.
  scope: data_type: predictive distributions
  extends B3: Gneiting, Balabdaoui and Raftery (2007) evaluate predictive distributions by maximising sharpness subject to calibration.
C14 [methodological; principal; extension; support: cited B13; uses C12; limitations L1, L5; strength moderate (author); planning exploratory; outcome supported]
  Predictive distributions of counts, such as forecasts of intermittent sales, should be evaluated with proper scoring rules and a randomised [[D18:probability integral transform]], since the probability integral transform of a discrete forecast is not uniform even when the forecast is calibrated.
  scope: data_type: predictive distributions of counts
  extends B13: Kolassa (2016) evaluates predictive count distributions with proper scoring rules and a randomised probability integral transform.
C15 [methodological; principal; extension; support: cited B2, at Section 3, Example 1 (Quadratic or Brier score); Section 4.1, cited B14; limitations L2, L3, L5; strength high (author); planning exploratory; outcome supported]
  Forecast probabilities of classes should be evaluated with a [[D2:proper scoring rule]], such as the [[D16:log score]] or the [[D17:Brier score]].
  scope: data_type: forecast probabilities of classes
  extends B2: Gneiting and Raftery (2007) show that the Brier and logarithmic scores are strictly proper.
C16 [methodological; principal; support: argument W4; uses C15; limitations L4, L5; strength moderate (author); planning exploratory; outcome supported]
  [[D21:accuracy]] should not be the measure of forecast class probabilities: it ignores the probabilities beyond which side of a threshold they fall, and weighs every error the same.
  scope: data_type: forecast class probabilities, thresholded to labels
C17 [methodological; principal; extension; support: cited B15; uses C15; limitations L1, L5; strength moderate (author); planning exploratory; outcome supported]
  [[D22:AUC]] should not be the measure of forecast class probabilities: it measures how well cases are ranked, not whether the probabilities are right, and it weighs the costs of misclassification differently for different classifiers.
  scope: data_type: forecast class probabilities, ranked by a classifier's scores
  extends B15: Hand (2009) shows that the AUC measures different classifiers with different misclassification cost distributions.
C18 [methodological; principal; extension; support: cited B8, at Title; Abstract; limitations L5; strength high (author); planning exploratory; outcome supported]
  With rare positive cases, the [[D23:precision-recall curve]] should be reported rather than the ROC curve alone, because the ROC curve can look good while most cases predicted positive are negative.
  scope: data_type: binary classifiers | conditions: imbalanced classes
  extends B8: Saito and Rehmsmeier (2015) show that the precision-recall plot is more informative than the ROC plot on imbalanced data.
C19 [methodological; principal; extension; support: cited B9, cited B1, at Section 5 (Discussion) (arXiv:0912.0902 numbering); uses C1; limitations L2, L3, L5; strength high (author); planning exploratory; outcome supported]
  When the compared methods target different functionals (one the mean, another the median), each should be evaluated with a measure consistent for its own target, or the methods made to target the same functional, because a single measure favours the methods that target what it is consistent for.
  scope: data_type: point forecasts of methods that target different functionals
  extends B9: Kolassa (2020) shows that different error measures are minimised by different summaries of one predictive density.
C20 [methodological; principal; extension; support: cited B4, at Section 3.1 (Comparisons of two classifiers), cited B7, at Section 3 (Scaled errors), cited B19, at Point forecasts (WRMSSE); uses C26; limitations L3, L5; strength high (author); planning exploratory; outcome supported]
  When methods are compared over many series, their accuracy should be summarised by aggregates of a scaled measure across the series, the mean together with the median and the quartiles of [[D6:RMSSE]], [[D8:MASE]] or another scaled error, since a mean over series alone is sensitive to a few outlying ones.
  scope: data_type: comparisons of methods over many series
  extends B4: Demsar (2006) notes that averages over data sets are susceptible to outliers.
C21 [methodological; principal; extension; support: cited B4, at Abstract; Section 3.1; Section 3.2.2 (Friedman test, with the Nemenyi test); uses C20; limitations L5; strength high (author); planning exploratory; outcome supported]
  To tell whether several methods whose aggregate accuracy is similar differ significantly over many series or data sets, their ranks on each series should be tested with the [[D25:Friedman test]] followed by [[D26:Nemenyi test]] post-hoc comparisons, which do not assume that errors are comparable across series.
  scope: data_type: comparisons of several methods over many series or data sets
  extends B4: Demsar (2006) recommends the Friedman test with post-hoc tests to test whether several classifiers differ over many data sets.
C22 [methodological; principal; extension; support: cited B16; uses C20; limitations L1, L5; strength moderate (author); planning exploratory; outcome supported]
  To tell which methods differ significantly from the best one over many series, multiple comparisons with the best ([[D27:MCB]]) on ranks should be used, as in the statistical analysis of the M3 competition.
  scope: data_type: comparisons of methods over many series
  extends B16: Koning et al. (2005) test the M3 results with multiple comparisons with the best on ranks.
C23 [methodological; principal; extension; support: cited B17; limitations L1, L5; strength moderate (author); planning exploratory; outcome supported]
  The accuracy of two forecasts of the same series should be compared with the [[D28:Diebold-Mariano test]], which allows forecast errors that are serially correlated and not normally distributed.
  scope: data_type: two forecasts of one series
  extends B17: Diebold and Mariano (1995) propose a test of equal accuracy of two forecasts.
C24 [methodological; principal; extension; support: cited B9, cited B1, at Abstract; uses C1; limitations L2, L3, L5; strength high (author); planning exploratory; outcome supported]
  An error measure should not be chosen because a competition or a well-known study used it: the measure has to be consistent for what the compared methods predict.
  scope: data_type: evaluations of point forecasts
  extends B9: Kolassa (2020), on the M4 measures, argues that the best point forecast depends on the error measure.
C25 [methodological; principal; extension; support: cited B7, at Section 2.1 (Scale-dependent measures); Section 2.4 (Relative measures), cited B18; limitations L2, L3, L5; strength high (author); planning exploratory; outcome supported]
  Scale-dependent error measures ([[D5:RMSE]], [[D7:MAE]]) should not be summed or averaged across series on different scales: the series with the largest values then dominate the aggregate, whatever the forecasts of the other series.
  scope: data_type: point forecasts over series on different scales
  extends B7: Hyndman and Koehler (2006) state that scale-dependent measures should not be used to compare across data sets on different scales.
C26 [methodological; principal; extension; support: cited B7, at Section 3 (Scaled errors), cited B19, at Point forecasts (RMSSE), cited B10, argument W5; uses C1; limitations L2, L3, L5; strength high (author); planning exploratory; outcome supported]
  To aggregate the accuracy of point forecasts over series on different scales, each series' errors should first be scaled by a quantity of that series (as in [[D8:MASE]] and [[D6:RMSSE]], or relative to a benchmark method), and the scaled errors then averaged, choosing a scaling that keeps the measure consistent for what the forecasts target.
  scope: data_type: point forecasts over series on different scales
  extends B7: Hyndman and Koehler (2006) propose scaled errors as the standard approach for comparing accuracy across series on different scales.
C27 [methodological; principal; extension; support: cited B19, at Probabilistic forecasts (Scaled Pinball Loss), cited B11, argument W5; uses C9; limitations L2, L5; strength high (author); planning exploratory; outcome supported]
  When quantile forecasts are compared over series on different scales, each series' [[D12:pinball loss]] should be scaled by a quantity of that series, as the scaled pinball loss of the M5 uncertainty competition divides it by the in-sample mean absolute error of the one-step naive forecast.
  scope: data_type: quantile forecasts over series on different scales
  extends B19: The M5 competition scored quantile forecasts with the pinball loss scaled per series.
C28 [methodological; principal; extension; support: cited B20, cited B7, at Section 2.1 (Scale-dependent measures), argument W5; uses C10; limitations L2, L3, L5; strength high (author); planning exploratory; outcome supported]
  When prediction intervals are compared over series on different scales, each series' [[D13:interval score]] should be scaled by a quantity of that series, as the mean scaled interval score (MSIS) of the M4 competition does.
  scope: data_type: prediction intervals over series on different scales
  extends B20: The M4 competition scored prediction intervals with the mean scaled interval score.

WARRANTS
W1 [deductive; premises C2] Dividing a series' squared errors by a fixed positive number, such as the in-sample mean squared error of its one-step naive forecast, does not change which forecast minimises their expected value; so the RMSSE, like the RMSE, is lowest in expectation for the mean, while series on different scales can be averaged.
W2 [deductive] The sMAPE divides each absolute error by the mean of the absolute outcome and the absolute forecast; when the outcome and the forecast are both zero, that ratio is zero divided by zero, so the measure is undefined.
W3 [deductive; premises C10] Coverage counts only whether each outcome falls inside its interval; widening an interval never lowers its coverage, so coverage alone cannot penalise needless width, which the interval score does.
W4 [deductive; premises C15] Accuracy uses a forecast probability only through which side of a threshold it falls, and counts every misclassification as the same loss; so two forecasts with different probabilities but the same thresholded labels score the same, and unequal costs of errors are not reflected. A proper scoring rule rewards the probabilities themselves.
W5 [deductive; premises C1] Dividing a series' scores by a quantity of that series that does not depend on the forecasts being compared (an in-sample error of the naive forecast, or a benchmark's error) does not change which forecast minimises the expected score of that series; so the scaled measure keeps the target of the unscaled one, while series on different scales can be averaged.
